Images of the Russian Empire

For this project, I took Prokudin-Gorskii's glass plate negatives and turned them into color images by separating and aligning the three exposures.

Approach

Each plate is one tall grayscale image containing three exposures. From top to bottom, they are blue, green, and red. I split the plate into thirds and use the blue channel as the reference, then search for the shifts that line up the green and red channels with it. I report each shift as (x, y), with positive x moving right and positive y moving down.

To compare two possible alignments, I use normalized cross-correlation (NCC). I only score pixels in the region where both shifted images overlap. I also leave out 12% around the outside because the plate borders have dark strips, scanner marks, and other details that do not belong to the actual scene.

For the smaller JPEGs, I can simply try every shift in a [-15, 15] pixel window. That would be too slow for the large TIFF files, so I use an image pyramid. I repeatedly shrink the image with 2x2 averaging, find an approximate shift at the smallest level, and refine it as I move back up. On my computer, most of the full-size images took about eight seconds.

Emir was harder because the brightness changes a lot between color channels. For the final version, I aligned gradient magnitudes instead of the original pixel values. The colors may have different brightness, but the edges of clothing, buildings, and other objects still tend to appear in the same places.

Bells And Whistles

Edge-Based Alignment

Raw NCC assumes that matching objects have similar brightness in both channels, which is not always true. I computed a simple gradient magnitude using horizontal and vertical differences, then ran the same NCC search on those edge images. This worked especially well on Emir, where the blue and red exposures look very different.

Automatic Enhancement

After aligning the channels, I remove areas that became empty because of the shifts. I also look near the outside of the image for borders with strong color disagreement or nearly black and white values. Finally, I stretch the contrast using percentiles and apply gray-world white balance. I kept the cropping conservative because cutting off part of the photograph looked worse than leaving a little of the original border.

Failure Notes

I did not see a clear alignment failure in the final pyramid results. A few images still have colored edges, stains, or pieces of the original plate border. Those problems mostly come from the scans and my conservative cropping rather than from the channels being shifted incorrectly.
